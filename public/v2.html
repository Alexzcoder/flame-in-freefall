🔥 Why fire is different in space

On Earth, hot gas rises and pulls in fresh air: flames are tall and flicker. In microgravity nothing rises, so oxygen only arrives by slow diffusion or by the ventilation fans. Flames become small, round, cooler and slower, and can survive at oxygen levels or airflows where they would go out on Earth. Ground tests therefore can't fully predict a cabin fire.

🗺️ How to read the map

Each dot is one experiment run on the ISS or a Cygnus cargo ship. Horizontal = cabin pressure (log scale), vertical = oxygen. Colour = experiment family. Stars mark the ISS (sea-level air) and a planned Moon base (30 % O₂ at 10.2 psi). The dotted box is the range of planned exploration habitats. Empty space means nobody has tested it.

✅ How much to trust a point

Filled dot: atmosphere measured. Hollow dot: atmosphere not recorded, so we assumed ISS cabin air. Only 633 of 1,624 runs record what happened (ignition, extinction, burn time). Some burn times are censored: the run was stopped while still burning, so the time is a lower bound. The Scenario explorer and the assistant flag all of these.

Hollow markers = pressure or O₂ assumed (cabin air, not measured). Dotted box = planned habitat zone (25–40 % O₂, 8–12 psi): the cabin air expected in Moon and Mars habitats. Hover it for details.

🌍 Why this matters on Earth

The same physics governs fires in places where oxygen or pressure differ from normal air: aircraft cabins (pressurised to ~10.9 psi), hyperbaric oxygen chambers and home oxygen therapy (oxygen-enriched air, where fabrics ignite more easily), submarines and clean rooms (low-flow ventilation). Microgravity removes buoyancy, so it isolates effects that are hidden on Earth: radiation losses, slow diffusion, the effect of low airflow. NASA's droplet and soot research also feeds cleaner, more efficient engines and burners. Try the Earth presets in the Scenario explorer.
